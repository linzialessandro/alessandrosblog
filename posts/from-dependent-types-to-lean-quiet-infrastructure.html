From Dependent Types to Lean: How Years of Quiet Maintenance Built Math's Proof Checker

November 23, 2026
MathematicsLeanFormalVerificationAIResearch

Between September and November 2026, Lean became the common checker behind several multi-million-line automated math releases. Anthropic reported an 11-day, 13-million-line formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem in September. [1] Axiom reported a 43.1-million-line formalization of the classification of finite simple groups in October and a 16-million-line Stacks Project release in November. [2] On 20 November 2026, Levent Alpöge released a 17-million-line Lean library claiming a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. Within a day, the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative certified that the formal proof compiles and matches Mathlib's statement. [3]

That verification layer did not appear in 2026. It rests on dependent type theory from the late 1960s through the 1980s. It depends on a theorem prover started in 2013 and rewritten from scratch starting in 2018. Most of all, it relies on nearly a decade of quiet library maintenance done before AI labs cared about formal proofs.

Dependent type theory and the de Bruijn criterion

Proof assistants like Lean build on two ideas from logic and programming languages. The first is the Curry-Howard correspondence, named after Haskell Curry's work on combinatory logic in the 1950s and William Alvin Howard's 1969 manuscript, published in 1980. [4] Under this correspondence, a mathematical proposition is a type, and a proof is a program that produces a value of that type. Checking a proof reduces to type checking a program.

Simple type systems cannot express quantifiers over values, such as "for every natural number n". Dependent type theory removes that limit by letting types depend on values. Across papers from 1971 to 1984, Per Martin-Löf formulated intuitionistic type theory with inductive definitions, dependent function types (Pi-types, mapping each x : A to a type B x) for universal statements, and dependent pair types (Sigma-types, pairing an x : A with a value in B x) for existential statements. [5] Type theorists also split between extensional and intensional equality. An intensional system uses decidable computation rules for definitional equality, which keeps type checking decidable while requiring users to prove non-computational equalities explicitly.

The second foundation came from N. G. de Bruijn's Automath project at Eindhoven, which ran from 1967 to 1980 and built the first working computer proof checker. [6] In 1977, L. S. van Benthem Jutting used Automath to check Edmund Landau's textbook Grundlagen der Analysis. [7] Automath introduced nameless variable binding, now called de Bruijn indices, and a design rule later named the de Bruijn criterion by Henk Barendregt. [6] A proof assistant meets this criterion if its automation emits explicit proof terms checked by a small, independent program called the kernel. You do not need to trust the search engine that found a proof. You only need to trust the small checker that inspects the final proof term.

In 1984 and 1985, Thierry Coquand and Gérard Huet combined polymorphic lambda calculus with dependent types in the Calculus of Constructions, drawing directly on Automath. [8] Christine Paulin-Mohring and Coquand added inductive types in 1988 to define the Calculus of Inductive Constructions. [5] That calculus became the foundation for Coq, started at INRIA in 1984, first released in 1989, and renamed The Rocq Prover in March 2025. [9] Coq proved that dependent type theory could scale to large projects, including Georges Gonthier and Benjamin Werner's 2005 formalization of the Four Color Theorem, the CompCert verified C compiler, and the Feit-Thompson theorem in 2012. In parallel, Ulf Norell's 2007 Chalmers thesis made Agda a practical dependently typed language built on Martin-Löf type theory, while Isabelle/HOL applied the same small-kernel discipline to higher-order logic. [10]

Leonardo de Moura and the Lean 4 rewrite

Lean did not start as an AI math platform. Leonardo de Moura, who had created the Z3 SMT solver at Microsoft Research, started Lean with Soonho Kong in July 2013 at Microsoft Research. [11] Their early aim was to bridge interactive and automated proving and build a shared library for tools such as Dafny, F*, Coq, and Isabelle. [12]

Early releases tested several directions. Lean 0.1 shipped in June 2014, and Lean 2 followed in 2015 with both standard dependent type theory and a built-in Homotopy Type Theory (HoTT) mode carrying a roughly 30,400-line library. [13] Lean 3, released in January 2017, dropped built-in HoTT mode and let users write tactics and metaprograms in Lean itself instead of C++. [12] Its logic is a variant of the Calculus of Inductive Constructions with an impredicative, proof-irrelevant proposition universe (Prop), quotient types (Quot.sound), propositional extensionality (propext), and classical choice (Classical.choice).

As Lean 3's user libraries grew, performance and extensibility limits pushed de Moura and Sebastian Ullrich to begin a full rewrite, Lean 4, in April 2018. [14] Their CADE 28 paper describes Lean 4 as both a theorem prover and an optimizing functional programming language. [15] Rather than keeping the parser, elaborator, compiler, and tactic framework in C++, they wrote Lean 4 in Lean itself, reaching over 120,000 lines of Lean by 2024. [11] To make pure functional code fast enough to compile itself, they built a reference-counting runtime ("Counting Immutable Beans", later generalized as Perceus) that mutates unshared objects in place. [12] The trusted proof checker stayed small and separate, honoring de Bruijn's rule while opening the rest of the toolchain to custom automation.

Mathlib and the unglamorous work nobody watched

While Lean 4 was taking shape as a compiler and language, a growing community built Lean's mathematics library, Mathlib. Factored out of Lean 3's core in July 2017 and initially led by Mario Carneiro and Johannes Hölzl at Carnegie Mellon University, Mathlib made an unusual organizational choice. [16] Instead of letting separate teams publish incompatible libraries with conflicting definitions, contributors kept one monolithic, centrally reviewed repository.

Keeping one library consistent required patient engineering. In a 2020 paper published under "The mathlib Community" (then 73 contributors), the authors laid out design decisions that look minor at small scale. [16] They used semi-bundled typeclasses so typeclass inference could find canonical structures without triggering higher-order matching blowups in the simplifier simp. They built bundled morphisms, used the @[to_additive] attribute to duplicate multiplicative lemmas for additive structures automatically, and maintained core decision tactics such as ring, linarith, and norm_num. Every pull request passed automated linters and human review so that analysis, algebra, and topology could share one set of definitions.

A teaching and coordination ecosystem grew around the code. The community opened its Zulip chat in February 2018, reaching over 5,000 members in November 2021 and over 10,000 in September 2024. [14] At Imperial College London, Kevin Buzzard's Xena project brought pure mathematicians into formalization, and in October 2019 Buzzard and Mohammad Pedramfar released the Natural Number Game to teach Lean tactics in a web browser. [17] Patrick Massot wrote the Blueprint tool, connecting LaTeX proof outlines directly to live dependency graphs of Lean declarations.

Then came the hardest stretch of invisible maintenance: porting Mathlib from Lean 3 to Lean 4. Lean 4 broke backwards compatibility with Lean 3. [18] Although the mathlib4 repository was created in May 2021, the bulk of the port ran from December 2022 to mid-July 2023. Volunteers ran an automated translator called mathport and then manually fixed syntax, tactics, and typeclass conflicts across more than 3,000 files and over one million lines of code. [19] When the port was declared complete on 15 and 16 July 2023, almost nobody outside the community cared. Without those six months of unglamorous porting, Lean 4 would have launched without a mathematical library.

From 1.25 million lines of Lean 4 code at the July 2023 port, Mathlib grew to 1.4 million lines by February 2024 while cutting build instructions by 40 percent. [20] By October 2026, de Moura reported Mathlib at 2.5 million lines of code. [21] The project's public statistics page counts 142,131 definitions, 296,355 theorems, and 772 contributors, and all 100 theorems on Freek Wiedijk's classic formalization list are now in Lean. [22] [23]

Research milestones and the Lean FRO

Before AI labs focused on Lean, human teams ran large formalization projects that tested whether Mathlib could handle current research mathematics.

In December 2020, Fields Medalist Peter Scholze challenged the formalization community to verify Theorem 9.4 of his analytic geometry work with Dustin Clausen. Led by Johan Commelin with major work by Adam Topaz and collaborators, the Liquid Tensor Experiment verified a key intermediate lemma in mid-2021, catching an oversight that Scholze said walked a fine line, and completed the full theorem on 14 July 2022. [24] [11] Later in 2022, Patrick Massot, Floris van Doorn, and Oliver Nash formalized the local h-principle in differential topology and deduced Stephen Smale's sphere eversion theorem, proving that continuous geometric arguments were also within reach. [25]

In November 2023, Timothy Gowers, Ben Green, Freddie Manners, and Terence Tao posted a preprint proving Marton's Polynomial Freiman-Ruzsa conjecture. Tao, Yaël Dillies, Bhavik Mehta, and collaborators formalized the entire proof in Lean 4 in about three weeks, finishing on 5 December 2023. [26] [27] Tao noted that Massot's Blueprint allowed contributors who had never met to formalize isolated lemmas in parallel because Lean's kernel checked every pull request. Tao followed that in September 2024 with the Equational Theories Project, completed in Lean in December 2025. [14] Meanwhile, Buzzard launched the Fermat's Last Theorem project in December 2023, later backed by a five-year 934,043.32 pound EPSRC grant running through September 2029 to formalize modern number theory and arithmetic geometry in Mathlib. [28]

Core Lean engineering also gained permanent backing in 2023. In July 2023, de Moura and Ullrich co-founded the Lean Focused Research Organization (Lean FRO) under Convergent Research, with initial support from the Simons Foundation International, the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, and the Richard Merkin Foundation. [14] Lean 4.0.0 shipped on 8 September 2023, starting a monthly release cadence that reached Lean 4.35.0 in October 2026. The FRO built the lake build system, the Reservoir package index, the Verso documentation tool, a new compiler, and stronger built-in automation such as the SMT-style grind tactic. In July 2025, Alex Gerko announced 10 million dollars in new funding, split evenly between the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative. [14]

When AI needed a verifier, Lean was ready

Machine learning researchers started connecting language models to Lean years before the 2026 wave. In 2021, Jesse Michael Han and collaborators at OpenAI and universities introduced Proof Artifact Co-training (PACT), extracting self-supervised training tasks from Lean 3 kernel proof terms. [29] In 2023, Kaiyu Yang and co-authors released LeanDojo to let models retrieve premises from Mathlib and interact programmatically with Lean 4. [30]

From 2024 onward, that pairing scaled quickly because Lean gave reinforcement learning an exact reward signal that natural-language math lacks. In July 2024, Google DeepMind's AlphaProof paired reinforcement learning in Lean with AlphaGeometry 2 to score 28 out of 42 points on the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad, a silver-medal performance later published in Nature in November 2025. [31] In April 2025, DeepSeek released DeepSeek-Prover-V2, an open-source Lean 4 prover reaching an 88.9 percent pass rate on MiniF2F-test. [32] In October 2025, Harmonic published Aristotle, combining Monte Carlo graph search in Lean 4 with informal lemma generation to solve five of six problems on the 2025 IMO without sorryAx, reaching gold-medal level. [33]

In late 2026, AI labs moved from olympiad problems to multi-million-line mathematical libraries built on Mathlib. Anthropic reported in September 2026 that Claude agents coordinated by Columbia University's Prove2Me dependency graph produced a 13-million-line Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in 11 days. The run used 29,500 intermediate theorems and consumed about 6 billion output tokens. [1] Axiom Math reported in October 2026 that agent swarms formalized the classification of finite simple groups in 43.1 million lines over 24 days, recording 34 noteworthy deviations in the published literature. [2] In November, Axiom followed with a 16-million-line formalization of the Stacks Project, which we examined in an earlier review. OpenAI published Lean certificates for forced Navier-Stokes blowup in September 2026 and released 722 math papers in October, with roughly 45 percent accompanied by Lean code. [34] On 20 November 2026, Levent Alpöge released a 17-million-line Lean library across 175,187 modules claiming a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. [35]

Scale also exposed risks inside Lean's own checker. On 15 October 2026, de Moura wrote that AI agents had found soundness bugs in Lean's C++ kernel itself. [21] Lean's fix returned directly to the de Bruijn criterion. Lean 4.35.0 introduced lake check and lake check --paranoid to export proofs and re-check them in a sandbox across independent kernels: the standalone leanchecker, the Rust checker nanoda, lean4lean, con-leche (a Lean kernel written in Lean and proven consistent relative to set theory under Joachim Breitner's supervision), and con-ron (a Rust translation verified against con-leche using Aeneas). [21]

Why the quiet work decided what could be trusted

The November 2026 Riemann Hypothesis verification shows how these pieces work together, and where their guarantees stop. Within 24 hours of Alpöge's release, MIT number theorist Andrew Sutherland and the Lean and Mathlib communities did not read 17 million lines of code. Instead, they rebuilt the archive in a network-isolated sandbox and reproduced the 61.6-gigabyte proof export byte for byte. They verified that the five headline theorems depend only on propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound, checked with comparator and con-leche --compare that the proved statements match Mathlib's human-vetted RiemannHypothesis definition, and ran the export through leanchecker, nanoda, con-leche, con-ron, and lean4lean. [3] [35]

Compare that with the failure modes documented in other 2026 releases. As we noted in our review of OpenAI's Navier-Stokes certificates, researchers Bastounis, Circelli, and Hansen showed that Lean files can type-check cleanly while proving weaker intermediate bounds than the accompanying PDF claims. In his 12 November 2026 checklist, Kevin Buzzard reported that about 38 of 290 checked Lean repositories in OpenAI's October dump did not state the paper's headline theorem. He also noted that Axiom's classification statement imports roughly 5,600 lines of unvetted definitions outside Mathlib, including sporadic simple groups not yet in Mathlib. [34] And as Columbia's LEVER project illustrates, kernel acceptance says nothing about whether a proof is concise, modular, or legible to humans.

That boundary explains why the quiet years before 2024 mattered so much. Lean did not become the verification engine for AI mathematics because it invented dependent type theory, which dates back to Martin-Löf, de Bruijn, Coquand, Huet, and Paulin-Mohring. It became useful because two groups spent years on work that rarely draws headlines. De Moura, Ullrich, and the Lean FRO kept a small checkable kernel separate from an extensible language, and later turned that separation into multi-kernel checking. Meanwhile, hundreds of Mathlib contributors spent years refining typeclass hierarchies, reviewing pull requests, fixing diamond bugs, and porting over a million lines from Lean 3 to Lean 4 so that core mathematical definitions sit in one vetted library.

When AI systems began emitting millions of lines of candidate proofs that no human referee could read line by line, an auditable kernel alone was not enough. Checking a proof still requires trusting the definitions in the theorem statement. The years of unglamorous maintenance built both halves of that boundary: small checkers that reject invalid proofs, and a shared, human-reviewed library that defines what those proofs actually say.
