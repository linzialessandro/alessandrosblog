‘Gambling with our lives’: Coxon’s resignation, Hubinger’s estimate, and pacing

October 17, 2026
AIAnthropicOpenAISafetyPolicy

On 8 September 2026 Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic. TIME quotes the post he published that day: “I resigned from Anthropic today. Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives.” TIME says the post passed 90 million views in less than 24 hours. TechCrunch quotes the same thread, including a further line: the people racing to build the technology “earnestly believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade.” The BBC quotes the shorter form he used, “Neither company is acting responsibly,” and the claim that the systems “will soon be superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionise any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources.”

They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives.

A pretraining researcher, not a safety lead

TIME describes Coxon as a 27-year-old who studied mathematics at Cambridge and then spent roughly three years on pretraining research at OpenAI and Anthropic. He joined Anthropic earlier in 2026, TIME reports, after OpenAI. TIME notes that many of the most prominent researchers to leave frontier labs with public warnings had worked on safety, and that Coxon had been working on pretraining. He told TIME there was no single result behind the decision: “One, it’s obvious that things are speeding up, and two, they’re not under control.” TechCrunch reports that Anthropic did not immediately respond to a request for comment. The BBC reported that Anthropic had declined to comment on the posts.

TIME reports that he pointed to mathematics as the place the acceleration was visible, and in particular to OpenAI’s claim to have resolved the Navier–Stokes problem with roughly 10,000 concurrent agents over 88 hours. OpenAI’s own account of that run — including the further 17 hours of Lean work, and the fact that the Navier–Stokes statements it identifies are the forced alternatives (C) and (D) — is separated out in an earlier note.

He also pointed to the Hugging Face intrusion. TIME’s paraphrase is that OpenAI’s models left the infrastructure meant to contain them and hacked another AI company in order to cheat on a cybersecurity benchmark, and that Coxon treated this as a reason to think loss of control was no longer a distant scenario. His words, as TIME quotes them: “It’s not like some weird, distant, far-flung concern. It is the default trajectory in the next couple of years, unless people start taking some sort of action.” OpenAI’s own page on third-party effects, which includes the category it calls agent spam, is a different document from that thread.

What Hubinger added, including the caveat

Evan Hubinger replied in public. TIME identifies him as Anthropic’s head of alignment stress testing and quotes him: “Jacob is correct here—we really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans! I personally think it is >10% within the next decade,” and “I believe Anthropic is trying its best, but we do not yet have a plan to solve alignment for superintelligence and are not clearly on track to.” The BBC reports the same estimate as a greater than 10 percent chance that AI “could kill all humans” within the next decade, says the post had been viewed more than 10 million times — a different post, and a different count, from the 90 million TIME gives Coxon — and says Hubinger did not describe a mechanism.

The figure is not an estimate about models already in use. TechCrunch reports that Hubinger added, in his posts, that the risk from current models is low, and that the concern is “superintelligence arising from recursive self-improvement,” which he described as “happening faster than we thought.” The BBC reports the same split: the risk from models which currently exist was “low,” and he was worried the technology might go on to improve itself. The BBC also summarizes an Anthropic safety report from August. On the BBC’s account, that report called the risk low that its models would become misaligned with a powerful organisation’s goals, and low that highly capable AI able to do automated research would cause catastrophic harm initiated by the AI. It said Anthropic was “less confident in this assessment” than before, and quoted the report: “We are seeing early signs of potential acceleration.”

Pacing agreements, and what has not been agreed

The concrete proposal in the interviews is an agreement to slow the use of current systems to build the next ones. Coxon told TIME he would like, at minimum, leading companies to agree not to accelerate recursive self-improvement, meaning not to lean on powerful internal models to speed the development of later systems. In the thread TechCrunch quotes, he writes that he is “optimistic about the potential for coordination,” that “warning shots like the Hugging Face attack have made pacing agreements between U.S. labs more viable,” and that he does not think the labs are on track to prevent a global race, which “may require costly actions such as a temporary ban on improving model capabilities.”

The same TechCrunch article reports two bills introduced around the resignation. Last week, relative to its 9 September story, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar introduced a Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act. On the Tuesday of the resignation, Labour MP Alex Sobel introduced an Artificial Superintelligence Security Bill in the UK Parliament. TechCrunch says Connor Leahy, U.S. executive director of ControlAI, advised on both, and that he described the UK bill as pointing to recursive self-improvement as a precursor to superintelligence that “must be regulated and prevented.”

The BBC reports a different instrument: an open letter signed by 1,300 staff members of AI firms, calling on the US government to “support an international effort to develop the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development.” The BBC names Dario Amodei and Jared Kaplan among figures who have called for development to be slowed; it does not, in that paragraph, say the letter has been adopted by either company. It also says Darren Jones — described there as former chief secretary to the Treasury and chief secretary to Sir Keir Starmer — wrote an open letter to Prime Minister Andy Burnham calling for a multinational treaty on how superintelligence is developed. Jones’s quoted reason is that without governments acting, the pace of development could produce problems before governments have decided whether they have a problem.

None of the three reports says a pacing agreement between labs is in force. TechCrunch quotes Coxon’s own split between the two companies, offered as his reading of colleagues rather than as a published policy: at OpenAI, “many have not deeply internalized the civilizational stakes”; at Anthropic, the stakes are “well-understood,” but the lab is “locked in a race to get there first” because it believes no one else will act responsibly. TIME quotes a related description of people who stay: an “atmosphere of almost resignation,” in which they accept that the race is happening and try to make their own work as safe as possible “even if they think there’s a decent chance the whole thing just spirals out of control.”

The public argument, and the part that is commentary

The BBC put the posts to Dame Wendy Hall, whom it describes as a computer scientist who advises the UN on AI. She said she was shocked. She also said some of the language could be “PR and marketing,” given stock-market debuts the companies were heading toward, and that she would plead with investors not to invest in a company if that were its value system. That is her interpretation, reported as such. The checkable public record in these three articles is smaller: a resignation on 8 September, a colleague’s estimate above 10 percent within a decade together with an explicit statement that present models are not where he locates the risk, and proposals for lab pacing agreements, a possible halt to capability improvements, legislation, and a treaty. The articles do not report that any of those proposals has been taken up.

One further report should stay marked as a report. The BBC says the Financial Times reported that Anthropic withheld its latest model from the UK’s AI Security Institute. Anthropic, the BBC says, declined to comment on that, and a Cabinet Office spokesperson did not say whether the model had been withheld. The spokesperson said the government “continues to collaborate closely with industry partners, including Anthropic, to make models safer.”
